# Portfolio, proof, and interview preparation

## Part 1 — fix the six assets you already have

You have six things: three campaigns and three creative projects. Finishing these beats
starting anything new. Ranked by what each does for your application.

---

### Campaign 1 — the 1,064-event campaign ($596.63, $0.56, 2.87% CTR)
**Your flagship. Build this one first.**

**What it must prove:** that you can run a campaign at volume and read what the numbers
actually mean.

**Supporting evidence needed:** the Ads Manager screenshot showing spend, results, cost per
result, CTR (all) — *with the date range and attribution window visible in the frame*, and
the columns not cropped. A recruiter who sees a cropped screenshot assumes something was
cropped out.

**The missing data that matters most:** what the Purchase event fired on. Then: AOV,
confirmation rate, delivery rate. If you have none of those, say so — see below.

**How to present the COD gap honestly.** Write this, or something close to it, directly on
the case study:

> These are Meta-reported purchase events. In this COD funnel the Purchase event fires on
> order-form submission, not on payment — so the figure is cost per order submitted, before
> confirmation and delivery attrition. Delivered-order and margin data sat with the
> fulfilment side and I did not have access to it. I have shown the number I can evidence,
> with the label it deserves.

That paragraph is the most valuable thing you can put in your portfolio. It costs you a
flattering headline number and buys you the one thing junior candidates cannot fake:
demonstrated understanding of what a platform metric is worth. Interviewers remember it.

---

### Campaign 3 — the 61.08% hook rate campaign ($491.15, $2.14, 2.29% link CTR)
**Second priority. This is the bridge between your two target roles.**

**What it must prove:** that a creative decision you made produced a measurable result.

**Supporting evidence needed:** the creative itself (the actual video), alongside the
breakdown showing hook rate and link CTR.

**Missing data that matters:** what you tested it against. A 61.08% hook rate in isolation
is a number; a 61.08% hook rate that beat two other openings you can name is a decision.
Even if the comparison is from memory, write it as memory: *"the two earlier openings I
tried were X and Y; this one held attention where they did not."* Honest recall beats a
number with no story.

**Note the cost per event is $2.14 here versus $0.56 and $0.28 on the others.** Do not hide
that. Explain it — different product, different price point, different market, whatever the
truth is. Being able to explain why one campaign cost four times another is exactly the
analytical fluency being screened for.

---

### Campaign 2 — the 441-event campaign ($125.02, $0.28, 3.24% CTR)
**Third. Useful but thin at $125 spend.**

**What it must prove:** consistency — that campaign 1 was not luck.

**Present it as a supporting exhibit inside campaign 1's case study rather than as its own
page.** A standalone case study on $125 of spend invites the budget question you are trying
to manage. As a second data point under a bigger one, it strengthens the story.

**Do not combine the three into a total.** $1,213 spend / 1,734 purchases / $0.70 blended
is arithmetically correct and presentationally dishonest without matching date ranges and
the same event definition. If a recruiter asks for a total, give the three separately and
say why.

---

### COBA Abu Dhabi — reel and proposal
**What it must prove:** that you can take a brief to a finished, strategically-reasoned deliverable.

**First, settle its status** — delivered client work, paid pitch, or unsolicited spec. Label
it exactly. An unconfirmed "client project" that turns out to be spec is the kind of thing
that unravels in a reference conversation.

**To make it demonstrate strategy rather than visual quality**, lead the description with
three sentences that have nothing to do with the edit: who the audience was, what the
single message needed to be, and why this format and length served that message. Then show
the reel. Most candidates show the reel and let it speak — it does not speak, it just looks
nice, and everyone's looks nice.

---

### Parker's — hospitality creative concept
**What it must prove:** self-directed creative thinking.

**Label it "independent concept, not commissioned"** in the title. And keep it structurally
separate from your Parker's employment everywhere — resume, LinkedIn, portfolio. Conflating
them looks like you are inflating a hospitality job into a marketing one, which is worse
than either fact on its own.

**Strongest framing:** you worked there, you saw the venue and its customers first-hand,
and you built a concept from that observation. That is a genuine insight-to-creative story
and it is the only one in your portfolio grounded in direct customer contact. Lead with it.

---

### Cinematic personal portfolio
**What it must prove:** craft ceiling.

**Keep it last, and keep it short.** It shows what you can make when nothing constrains you.
That is worth something, but it is the least relevant asset for either target role, and
placing it first signals that you think of yourself as a videographer.

### The two artefacts you do not have, and need

Both are for the creative strategist track, and both are a weekend each.

1. **One written creative brief.** For any of the three campaigns, retrospectively: the
   objective, the audience, the insight, the single message, the mandatories, three angle
   options, and which you chose and why. This is the core document of the job and you
   currently have none.
2. **One storyboard-to-final comparison.** Storyboard frames next to the finished cut, with
   a note on what changed between them and why. This proves you plan rather than improvise.

## Part 2 — the 45–60 second introduction

### Media Buyer version

> I'm a media buyer based in Dubai, working mainly in Meta Ads for e-commerce.
>
> I learned paid media from the hard side of it — cash-on-delivery e-commerce in Morocco,
> where I ran the whole thing myself: product research, sourcing, the landing page, the
> creative, the campaigns, and then confirmation, delivery and returns. What that taught me
> is that the number in Ads Manager is not the business result. In a COD funnel the purchase

> reading platform performance against what was actually delivered and what the margin was
> after fulfilment.
>
> My campaigns have been on modest budgets — the largest I can show you is about $600 of
> spend for around a thousand order submissions at $0.56 each. I'd rather show you real
> numbers with the right label on them than round ones I can't defend.
>
> The other half of what I do is creative. I write the hooks and scripts and cut the videos
> myself, so I'm not waiting on a production queue to test an idea.
>
> What I'm looking for now is a role where I can own accounts and grow into real budget
> responsibility — that's the main thing my experience doesn't have yet, and it's the reason
> I'm targeting this level rather than pitching myself above it.

*(~60 seconds. The last paragraph is deliberate: naming your own gap before they find it
converts your weakest point into evidence of self-awareness, and it pre-empts the "you seem
junior for this" objection by agreeing with it first.)*

### Creative Strategist version

> I'm a creative strategist and short-form video producer in Dubai, and the unusual part of
> my background is that I also buy the media — so I see which concepts survive contact with
> a cold audience, and which ones only looked good in the edit.
>
> Most of my work has been cash-on-delivery e-commerce, selling to people who've never heard
> of the brand and are being asked to commit to an order on the strength of the ad alone.

> What I'm building on now is documentation — briefs, storyboards, structured test matrices.

> part of the job I want to get formal about.

*(~60 seconds. Again, the closing names the real gap.)*

## Part 3 — likely interview questions

### Media Buyer — five questions and how to answer

**1. "What's the largest monthly budget you've managed?"**
The question you will be asked every single time. Do not inflate, do not deflect.
> "Honestly, small — my campaigns have been in the hundreds of dollars, the largest around
> $600. I haven't had a monthly budget of my own yet. What I have had is full ownership of
> the P&L around that spend, so I was making the same decisions a bigger budget needs, just
> with less room for error. Scaling the number is the part I need a role like this for."

**2. "Walk me through what you do when a campaign that was working starts to decline."**
Your LinkedIn already has the right instinct — make it concrete.
> Check whether it's delivery or conversion first. Rising frequency with falling CTR at
> stable CPM points to creative fatigue. Stable CTR with falling conversion rate points past
> the ad, to the page or the offer. CPM spiking with everything else flat points to auction
> or audience. Then change one thing. Then name what you did in a real instance.

**3. "Your cost per purchase is $0.56. What was the average order value?"**
The trap. It is not hostile — they are checking whether you know what your own number means.
> "Those are Meta-reported purchase events, and in that COD funnel the event fired on order
> submission rather than payment — so it's really cost per order placed, before confirmation
> and delivery attrition. [Then either the AOV and delivery rate, or:] I didn't have access
> to the delivered-order data, so I'd be guessing at a true CPA and I'd rather not."

**4. "How do you set up conversion tracking?"** — your weakest area. Do not bluff.
> "At the level I've worked at, I've used the Pixel with standard events and worked with
> what was already implemented rather than building the setup myself. GA4, GTM and CAPI are
> the clearest gap in my skill set and I'm working through them now — [say specifically
> what you're doing]. I'd rather tell you that than overstate it."

**5. "Why should we hire you over someone with three years of agency experience?"**
> Do not claim to be better. Claim to be different and cheaper to deploy: end-to-end
> commercial ownership including margin, which most three-year agency buyers have never
> touched; the ability to produce your own creative; Arabic and English. Then be direct
> about the trade-off — they get less scale experience and someone who will take
> instruction. That answer wins more often than a competitive one.

### Creative Strategist — five questions

**1. "Walk me through a creative that failed and what you learned."**
Have one ready. A candidate with no failures has no testing process.

**2. "How do you decide what to test next after a winning ad?"**
> Isolate what won — hook, angle, offer, format — then hold that constant and vary the next
> layer. Name the hierarchy you work down. Use the 61.08% campaign as the example.

**3. "What's your process from brief to finished ad?"**
> Research → angle → hook options → script → storyboard → produce → cut variants. Be honest
> that your briefs have historically been informal and you are formalising them.

**4. "How many concepts can you produce in a week?"**
Throughput matters here. Give a real number you can sustain, not an impressive one.

**5. "This is UGC-led — have you worked with creators?"**
Almost certainly no. Say so, and pivot to what transfers: scripting for a native, non-
polished register, and directing to a hook structure. Do not claim creator management.

## Part 4 — claims you must be ready to defend

Every one of these will be tested. If you cannot defend it, remove it from your materials
rather than hoping it goes unnoticed.

1. **What the Purchase event fired on.** Non-negotiable. Know it before you send anything.
2. **Every date on your LinkedIn.** If a duration is wrong, fix it now rather than explaining
   it under pressure.
3. **Your employment relationship with Digitalpole** — employee, freelance, or remote
   support — and whether you had live ad account access.
4. **Google Ads and TikTok Ads depth.** Your current headline promises Google at Meta's
   level. Either demote it (recommended) or be ready to discuss search campaign structure,
   match types and Quality Score.
5. **COBA's status.** Client, pitch, or spec.
6. **Parker's** — employment versus the independent creative concept. Two separate things.
7. **Certifications.** Once removed, if asked: *"I'm preparing for both, not certified yet."*
8. **Anything on your portfolio site.** Which is currently the strongest argument for not
   publishing it until the figures are real.
